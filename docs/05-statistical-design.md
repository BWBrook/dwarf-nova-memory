# Statistical design: predictive information in completed outbursts

**Status:** proposed research protocol, prepared 5 November 2026. This is a design to challenge and revise during a documented development phase. It is not a preregistration, a completed analysis, or evidence that any dwarf nova has a particular effect.

## 1. The question and the unit of inference

The first paper should ask:

> For a repeatedly observed dwarf nova, does information about its most recently completed outburst improve predictions of the next outburst beyond a renewal model and a model that adapts to changing activity?

The strongest initial version is a **historical, within-system forecast evaluation**, with partial pooling across a deliberately narrow set of systems. Duration is the first completed-outburst predictor. A homogeneous-band optical fluence proxy is a secondary predictor when its measurement is defensible. The target is an uncertainty distribution for the next onset, not just its expected date.

“Predictive memory” means that a specified piece of past information improves an honestly evaluated future prediction. It does not establish that one outburst causes the timing of the next. A completed outburst may measure an unobserved disc state more accurately than previous recurrence intervals do. This distinction should appear in the title, abstract and discussion of any eventual paper.

The following questions should remain separate:

| Question | Required comparison | What a positive result would support |
|---|---|---|
| Does a completed mark improve a stationary forecast? | M1 versus M0 | Incremental information relative to a stationary renewal baseline |
| Does activity change in a predictively useful way? | M2 versus M0 | Value of adapting to past changes in timing |
| Does the completed mark add to that adaptation? | M3 versus M2 | Incremental information beyond the specified changing-activity baseline |
| Is the mark effect direct rather than a measurement of latent state? | Joint mark–state models with and without a direct effect | At most model-dependent structural evidence; this may be unidentifiable |

The third row is the central scientific target. The fourth is an ancillary investigation, not a necessary promise for the first paper.

## 2. Define the events before fitting their relationships

For system \(j\), let outburst \(i\) have threshold-defined onset \(s_{ij}\), end \(e_{ij}\), and duration \(D_{ij}=e_{ij}-s_{ij}\). Define the subsequent **quiescent gap**

\[
Q_{ij}=s_{i+1,j}-e_{ij}.
\]

The onset-to-onset interval is \(C_{ij}=s_{i+1,j}-s_{ij}=D_{ij}+Q_{ij}\). Regressing \(C\) on \(D\) therefore introduces a shared component even when \(D\) and \(Q\) are physically independent. Do not make that regression the primary test. This issue already has antecedents in dwarf-nova work; see the literature review, especially Ak et al. (2002).

Using \(Q\) removes that algebraic coupling but does **not** remove measurement coupling. If the estimated end is \(\hat e=e+\delta\), then \(\hat D=D+\delta\) and \(\hat Q=Q-\delta\). Independent end-time error alone contributes \(-\operatorname{Var}(\delta)\) to their covariance. A negative duration–gap relation can thus be generated by uncertain boundaries. Propagate shared endpoint uncertainty jointly; independent error bars on duration and gap are insufficient.

The extraction protocol must record:

- Onset and end brackets, with the observations supporting each bound.
- The band, reference level and operational brightness threshold used to define the event.
- Whether an event is complete, partially observed, merged, or uncertain.
- The time at which its completion could first be established from observations available then.
- Whether the following gap is resolved, right-censored, or potentially contains missed outbursts.
- Any event-type assignment and the information used to make it. Types assigned using later outcomes cannot enter earlier forecasts.

Do not equate these threshold-defined endpoints with a unique physical moment in an accretion disc. Repeat the analysis over a small, prespecified set of defensible thresholds; changes in the scientific conclusion are a result, not an inconvenience to optimise away.

### The forecast is issued when the information becomes available

The true end \(e_i\) is generally not known at that instant. Let \(\tau_i\) be an **observable issue time**, for example the first sufficiently reliable confirmation that the system has returned to quiescence. The predictor ledger may use only observations at or before \(\tau_i\). A retrospective, smoothed estimate using later observations belongs in validation truth, not in the feature table.

When the end is known and \(a_i=\tau_i-e_i\), survival to issue time must be respected. Write \(\mathcal K_{\tau_i}\) for the available predictors and fitted history before imposing survival of the current gap to age \(a_i\). For horizon \(h\),

\[
\Pr(s_{i+1}\le\tau_i+h\mid Q_i>a_i,\mathcal K_{\tau_i})
=1-\frac{S_i(a_i+h\mid\mathcal K_{\tau_i})}{S_i(a_i\mid\mathcal K_{\tau_i})}.
\]

Here \(S_i\) describes the entire gap before the landmark conditioning. Incorporate survival to age \(a\) exactly once. With posterior mixtures, either take the ratio of marginal survivals using pre-landmark weights, or average conditional survival ratios using weights already updated for survival; do not mix those constructions. With an uncertain end, integrate over its joint conditional distribution with duration and other marks. If an unobserved outburst could have occurred and ended before confirmation, the assumption \(Q_i>a_i\) is not established: that episode needs a missing-event treatment or exclusion from the tightly monitored primary series.

The included synthetic demonstration uses an **oracle origin at the true end**, so \(a=0\). It deliberately does not implement this real-data landmark problem.

### Optical fluence is an observational mark

Within a homogeneous passband, a relative flux can be defined by \(f(t)=10^{-0.4[m(t)-m_{\rm ref}]}\). An integrated excess above a documented quiescent baseline supplies a useful optical fluence proxy. Preserve the integration window, baseline uncertainty, incomplete coverage, calibration offsets and nondetections. A missing wing is not zero flux; repeated truncation of noisy excess flux at zero can also bias an integral.

This quantity is not bolometric energy, accreted mass or a calibrated disc-reservoir measurement. Unfiltered measurements, visual estimates and different survey bands should not be put on one fluence scale using an assumed constant offset. Start with duration, then ask whether adequately measured fluence adds information.

## 3. A model ladder that remains tractable

Use a lognormal accelerated-failure-time model as the first parametric benchmark, with a Weibull or gamma renewal distribution as a prespecified shape sensitivity. These are statistical descriptions, not disc-instability simulations. A Poisson process is too restrictive to serve as the only null: a renewal process can have a refractory period or concentrated recurrence times without a dependence on the previous mark.

Let

\[
x_{D,ij}=\log D_{ij}-\overline{\log D}_{j,\mathrm{train}}.
\]

Keep the centring constant fixed from the applicable training set. Do not centre using the entire series. A pooled coefficient based on differences between stars answers a different question from a within-star coefficient. If between-star relationships are examined, enter the training mean as a separate predictor.

An initial hierarchy is:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{M0}:\quad &\log Q_{ij}=\alpha_j+\epsilon_{ij},\\
\mathrm{M1}:\quad &\log Q_{ij}=\alpha_j+\beta_{D,j}x_{D,ij}+\epsilon_{ij},\\
\mathrm{M2}:\quad &\log Q_{ij}=\alpha_j+z_j(\tau_{ij})+\epsilon_{ij},\\
\mathrm{M3}:\quad &\log Q_{ij}=\alpha_j+z_j(\tau_{ij})+\beta_{D,j}x_{D,ij}+\epsilon_{ij},
\end{aligned}
\]

where \(\epsilon_{ij}\sim N(0,\sigma_j^2)\). A common slope with sensitivity to individual slopes is a reasonable first implementation. Hierarchical slopes \(\beta_{D,j}\sim N(\beta_D,\tau_\beta^2)\) become useful when the number and quality of per-system cycles justify them. A population prior does not make a convenience sample representative of all dwarf novae.

For the initial changing-activity benchmark, compare a small number of **timing-only** alternatives selected in development: a rolling/discounted renewal fit, an autoregression in previous log gaps, and a filtered latent intercept. Use the same selected state architecture in M2 and M3. Do not permit M3 to win solely because it has a more flexible baseline.

A calendar-time Ornstein–Uhlenbeck state is one possible later implementation:

\[
z_j(\tau_i)\mid z_j(\tau_{i-1})
\sim N\!\left(e^{-\Delta_i/\ell}z_j(\tau_{i-1}),
\sigma_z^2[1-e^{-2\Delta_i/\ell}]\right),
\quad \Delta_i=\tau_i-\tau_{i-1}.
\]

All times in that update are known at the forecast origin. Treating the state as the intercept for the next gap is a local statistical approximation. A continuous-time state that changes throughout the future wait would require integrating the resulting hazard; do not silently interpret the simpler model as that process. An event-index AR(1) is another benchmark but encodes a different timescale from a calendar-time model.

The scale \(\ell\), state variance, measurement error and mark effect can be weakly identifiable. Use prior predictive simulation and sensible regularisation, then test recoverability. A model that can create a separate state for every event can absorb almost any apparent memory.

### The crucial limit of M3 versus M2

In this ladder, M2 uses timing history to infer activity. If duration is a better indicator of latent activity, M3 can legitimately improve forecasts even when duration has no direct effect on the following gap. Call that **incremental predictive information**, not evidence that latent-state confounding has been eliminated.

For a stronger structural extension, specify a joint model such as \(p(D_i\mid z_i)\,p(Q_i\mid z_i,D_i)\). Both competing models must allow the completed duration to inform the latent state; only the direct conditional duration effect differs. Establish in simulation whether the two explanations are distinguishable under actual cadence and noise. If not, report that non-identifiability explicitly.

## 4. Observation and censoring belong in the design

### Start with a restricted, auditable sample

The first implementation should use well-monitored episodes, with an explicit rule for when a sufficiently complete quiescent sequence begins and ends. Define the rule from cadence, depth and injection–recovery, not from whether a convenient next outburst was eventually seen. Keep all eligible issued forecasts; right-censor them when reliable coverage ends. Requiring a resolved subsequent outburst selectively removes long waits.

This estimates performance for the specified monitored systems and episodes. It does not estimate a population-wide recurrence relation. Report the eligible and excluded durations, event types, observing regimes and systems, so the changed target population is visible.

### Basic likelihood contributions

If the end is known, no intervening event was missed, and coarsening is ignorable conditional on modelled information, the contributions for a gap known to survive to age \(a\) are:

| Available outcome information | Conditional likelihood contribution |
|---|---|
| Exact event time \(q>a\) | \(f(q)/S(a)\) |
| Onset in \((l,u]\), with \(u>a\) | \([F(u)-F(\max\{l,a\})]/S(a)\) |
| No onset before censoring age \(c\ge a\) | \(S(c)/S(a)\) |

Evaluate small interval probabilities and tail differences stably in log space. If both endpoints are uncertain, these simple rows are conditional building blocks inside a joint integration. They are not permission to substitute independently sampled duration and gap values.

The first partial interval at the beginning of an archive is not a complete renewal interval. Either begin after a reliably observed completed outburst or explicitly model the initial age/residual-wait distribution. Distinguish left truncation, an unknown initial age, interval censoring, and a missing event; they require different treatments. Turnbull (1976) is a methodological starting point for grouped, censored and truncated data, not a complete solution to missed recurrent outbursts.

### A seasonal gap is not merely a large error bar on one event

Two detected outbursts can enclose zero, one or several undetected events. The elapsed time between them is then not an ordinary draw from the single-gap distribution. A thinned renewal process is generally not repaired by multiplying a rate by a scalar detection probability.

For ambiguous intervals, choose one of two defensible paths:

1. End the tightly monitored episode at coverage loss and resume only after the next independently confirmed event completion. Retain the resulting censoring and disclose the restricted estimand.
2. Fit a latent event/light-curve model that sums or integrates over possible missing events and their marks. This is a later extension, contingent on simulation demonstrating identifiability.

Do not impute a guessed number of outbursts by dividing a long interval by the fitted mean recurrence time and then fit those imputed events as data.

### Photometry and the observing process

A full model would generate a latent brightness trajectory, then observations conditional on time, band, calibration, noise and detection rules. A “fainter than” limit \(m_{\rm lim}\) has the numerical direction \(m>m_{\rm lim}\), or flux below the corresponding threshold. In a simple censored-measurement model this applies to the unreported noisy measurement, providing a probabilistic constraint on true flux; retain the provider's detection/significance convention. It is neither an exact measurement at the limiting magnitude nor zero flux. An absent row gives no information about whether the object was observed.

Fixed actual timestamps are useful for **conditional recovery experiments**. They do not prove that observation times are independent of brightness. Observers may increase effort after an alert or suspected rise. Conditioning on the entire recorded trigger history can sometimes make a sequential sampling policy ignorable, but incomplete trigger histories make that assumption doubtful. Compare scheduled survey observations with adaptive observations, simulate outcome-dependent effort, and narrow the conclusion if those regimes give different answers.

## 5. Retrospective forecasting without information leakage

Use rolling, common-calendar cutoffs. At cutoff \(T\), all population hyperparameters, normalisation, extraction tuning, thresholds and model choices use only the permitted development information. A gap begun before \(T\) but ending later is right-censored at \(T\) in the training data; do not sneak its later endpoint into the fit.

Within each subsequent evaluation block, parameters may remain frozen while latent states and observed histories are filtered forward. Alternatively, use a prespecified online refitting schedule. In both cases, forecasts may use only information available at issue time. Random event-level cross-validation and full-series state smoothing answer an easier problem than predicting the future. Bürkner, Gabry and Vehtari (2020) provide the relevant leave-future-out motivation.

Use three distinct stages:

1. **Development:** archive inspection, annotation, reproduction, extractor tuning and choice of a small model set.
2. **Validation:** earlier rolling forecast blocks used to choose hyperparameters and settle the analysis protocol.
3. **Locked final evaluation:** later blocks whose outcomes do not guide changes. Commit a protocol and outcome-access policy before opening them. If historical data have already been inspected, disclose that and choose another held-out interval or a prospective extension.

A common calendar cutoff matters for pooled models: fitting population hyperparameters using other stars' later observations can otherwise leak future information into a nominal historical forecast. Separately evaluate transfer to a new system only if that is an explicit secondary target; it is not the same as predicting the future of a monitored system.

Every forecast needs an immutable ledger containing `target_id`, issue time, model/version, parameter-training cutoff, latest input observation, known mark values and uncertainty, conditional survival distribution, and forecast horizons. Store the outcome evidence separately. Perturbing any later observation must leave the earlier forecast unchanged.

A reconstruction using observation dates but lacking archive ingestion/revision dates should be called an **observation-time hindcast**. It cannot establish that the same input data and classifications were operationally available at the historical date.

## 6. Score distributions, calibration and useful effect sizes

The primary comparison should be paired out-of-sample log predictive scores for the **same validation evidence**. With censoring or interval observations, score the corresponding evidence probability using the same observation/coarsening assumptions for all models. A common observation model is crucial: models should not gain credit by making the validation data less observable.

For \(J\) selected systems and \(n_j\) eligible forecasts per system, a transparent summary is

\[
\Delta_{3,2}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_j\frac{1}{n_j}\sum_i
\left[\log p_3(\mathcal O_{ij}\mid\mathcal H_{\tau_i})-
\log p_2(\mathcal O_{ij}\mid\mathcal H_{\tau_i})\right].
\]

Equal system weighting prevents the best-observed object from silently defining the main answer. Also report every system's score and a forecast-weighted operational summary. Treat repeated forecasts and cycles within a system as dependent. Use system-level resampling or a suitable hierarchical analysis, plus within-system temporal blocks where needed; do not bootstrap individual photometric measurements as if they were independent scientific replicates. With only a few systems, show the fragility of interval estimates and leave-one-system-out sensitivity.

Secondary diagnostics should include:

- Coverage and width of predictive intervals, with appropriate treatment of censored outcomes.
- Event probabilities at prespecified horizons; candidate development horizons are 7, 14 and 30 days, adjusted before final evaluation to fit the selected systems.
- Brier scores at horizons where event status is observable, or a justified censoring-aware estimator. Standard inverse-probability censoring weighting requires a valid conditional censoring model and adequate positivity; it does not fix missed events or informative sampling by itself.
- CRPS for complete, adequately measured waits. Do not replace a censored event time with its censoring time to compute ordinary CRPS.
- Calibration plots or probability-integral-transform diagnostics on appropriate complete/coarsened outcomes; do not expect naive uniform ranks after dropping unresolved long waits.

Proper scoring rules reward honest predictive distributions; Gneiting and Raftery (2007) provide the general foundation. Graf et al. (1999) provide a survival-data prediction-error reference, with assumptions that need to be revisited for these recurrent, incompletely observed events.

Report a physical-scale coefficient as well as score improvement. Under the unscaled log-duration formulation, doubling duration multiplies conditional median quiescent time by \(2^{\beta_D}\). The mark coefficient is a conditional association and its interpretation changes when state covariates change. A useful provisional simulation target is a 20% change in median wait for a doubling of duration; also test weaker effects and either sign. This number is a design choice to review with a domain astronomer, not an established astrophysical threshold.

## 7. Keep the first paper bounded

The minimum scientific deliverable is a reproducible observation-time hindcast study of a well-defined set of frequently outbursting systems, with documented event uncertainty, competing renewal and changing-activity baselines, and a simulation showing what its conclusions can distinguish. Start with six feasibility targets; expand only when the annotation, recovery and forecast protocols work.

The following are extensions rather than simultaneous requirements: a multi-band physical disc model, a survey-complete population study, subtype comparisons, superoutburst cycles, a multi-state hidden semi-Markov model, and telescope-allocation optimisation. Record interesting results as hypotheses for later work instead of letting them redefine the primary analysis after outcomes are seen.
